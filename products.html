What every score tells you

Four questions. One answer each.

Two are about the product. Two are about the money. Every score covers all four, with a country flag and a confidence percentage on each.

1

Made in

Where the physical product was manufactured. From labels, disclosures, customs records, and brand-family knowledge.

2

Ships from

Where the actual inventory sits before you buy it. Often a domestic fulfillment center regardless of manufacture.

3

Retailer

The company that takes your payment. First-party retailer, or a third-party marketplace seller with its own country of registration.

4

Money goes to

The parent company that ultimately receives the profit. Brand names roll up to holding groups; we surface the top of the chain.

How it works

Real evidence, honestly weighed.

We answer four questions about a product by pulling signals from many independent places — not just the retailer's own page. Claude AI (Anthropic's frontier model) reads all of it, weighs it, and produces the four scores with confidence bands. The AI never invents evidence — every citation on the score card links to a real source. The more sources agree, the more confident we are. When they don't agree, we say so.

Where we look

No single source decides a score. On every product we can, we consult:

Signals we combine on every score

  • The retailer's product page (title, brand, "Country of Origin" field, buy-box lines)
  • The seller's registered business details and country of incorporation
  • The brand's storefront and its official website
  • The public global domain registry (RDAP) for the brand's website
  • Our 3.8M-entry USPTO trademark index
  • Customer reviews and Q&A for made-in claims and shipping mentions
  • Our own database of previously verified corrections
  • Live web search across business registries, brand sites, and news

How the AI puts it together

The assembled evidence is sent to Anthropic's Claude AI, which weighs it against its own trained knowledge of brands and companies — it knows Bosch is German, Hydro Flask is American, TCL is Chinese. Before Claude looks at the structured signals, we require it to write down what it already knows about the brand. That protects against paper trails: a Chinese seller registering "BRAND INC" in Delaware would otherwise fool a naive trademark lookup into reporting "United States." When world knowledge and paperwork disagree, world knowledge wins.

The strongest evidence always wins. A literal "Country of Origin" field on the product page beats a guess from the brand name. An independent registry record beats a hunch. And a company's headquarters can never, by itself, be used to claim where a product is manufactured — a common and misleading conflation we refuse to make.

How confidence works

Each indicator ships with two numbers: a percent (our certainty for the top country) and a confidence (our overall confidence in the whole estimate). These are not the same, and the distinction is the point.

  • Percent 90 · Confidence 90 — strong, multiple corroborating sources. Trust it.
  • Percent 80 · Confidence 50 — a reasonable lean, but sparse evidence. Working hypothesis.
  • Percent 50 · Confidence 20 — we cannot tell. The number is a placeholder; the low confidence is the signal.
→
We won't raise confidence above 85 unless we have a literal retailer-supplied origin field or multiple independent web sources confirming the same answer. Confidence honesty is the design principle.

Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan

For all four indicators, we score Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan as their own countries — never folded into mainland China. This is a hard rule.

When you spot a wrong score

Every score has a Contest this finding button. Tell us what's wrong and (if you have one) attach a source — a manufacturer's page, an official disclosure, a photo. Our review team verifies every claim. When we agree, the correction becomes the new answer for every future shopper, marked ✓ Verified. The dataset gets better because you tell us when it isn't.

Bottom line

Our scores are well-supported probabilistic estimates, not factual claims. When evidence is strong, we are confident. When evidence is thin, we say so loudly. Every source is cited, and every citation is a link you can click.
